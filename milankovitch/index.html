How it works

Milutin Milanković, a Serbian mathematician, astronomer and engineer, worked out in the early 1900s that regular changes in Earth's orbit pace the ice ages.

Eccentricity

≈ 100,000 & 413,000 yr

How stretched the orbit is, from nearly circular (0.005) to slightly oval (0.058). Today it is about 0.017.

Alone it changes total sunlight by only a fraction of a percent, but it controls how strongly precession can matter.

Tilt (obliquity)

≈ 41,000 yr

The angle of Earth's axis ranges between about 22.1° and 24.5°. Today it is 23.4° and slowly decreasing.

More tilt means stronger seasons and more summer sunlight at high latitudes, which helps melt ice.

Precession

≈ 19,000–23,000 yr

A slow wobble, like a spinning top, plus a rotating orbit. It decides which season happens when Earth is closest to the Sun.

Today Earth is closest in early January (northern winter). About 11,000 years ago that was reversed.

Why watch summer at 65°N?

Large continents sit at those latitudes in the north (Canada, Scandinavia, Siberia). If summers are cool, winter snow survives, piles up and slowly becomes ice. Once ice covers the land it reflects sunlight and cools things further. This is a feedback that amplifies the small orbital nudge. Milanković's key idea: it's the summer sunlight at high northern latitudes that decides whether ice sheets grow or retreat.

Explore & think

1. Which single cycle changes summer sunlight at 65°N the most?

Switch to Only: P, then Only: E, then Only: T and compare the insolation chart. Precession gives the biggest swings every ~21,000 years, tilt gives a slower, steadier 41,000-year rhythm, and eccentricity on its own barely moves it. Eccentricity matters mostly because it scales precession's effect.

2. Which combination best matches the ice-age pattern?

No single cycle does, and the combination of all three gets only part of the way. The real ice record is dominated by a ~100,000-year rhythm, even though 100,000-year eccentricity is the weakest direct forcing. Scientists call this the "100,000-year problem". Feedbacks involving ice-sheet dynamics, ocean circulation and greenhouse gases (CO₂) are thought to amplify and pace the response. Try the Model vs. the Ice-age pattern line in the Ice chart.

3. If only orbits controlled climate, what would happen next?

Drag the slider to +50,000 yr. Eccentricity is heading toward a very low value, which flattens the precession swings, so northern summers stay relatively warm for a long time. Published studies suggest orbital forcing alone would not bring the next glacial period for tens of thousands of years, and that today's greenhouse-gas levels could delay it further. Orbits set the pacing, but they are not the only control.

4. Today's warming is not an orbital effect. How can you tell?

Over the last century, orbital changes have been tiny and, if anything, a very slight cooling push. Move the slider between −5,000 yr and +5,000 yr: the orbital curves barely move on the scale of a human lifetime, while observed warming over 150 years is very fast.

About the model & its limits

  • Eccentricity, tilt and precession are simplified sums of the main periodic terms, tuned to match today's values and typical ranges. For research-grade values use published solutions (e.g. Laskar et al. 2004; Berger & Loutre).
  • Insolation is the daily-mean sunlight at the June solstice, calculated from the standard formula, at the chosen latitude (default 65°N).
  • The ice "Model" is a deliberately simple toy: ice grows slowly when summer sunlight is low and melts faster when it is high. Its settings were tuned by hand to resemble the ice-age rhythm, so treat it as qualitative: it has no CO₂ or ocean feedbacks.
  • The "Ice-age pattern" is a schematic saw-tooth built from the approximate dates of the last nine glacial terminations (≈14, 130, 248, 337, 424, 533, 621, 710 and 790 thousand years ago). It is not measured data. To compare against real records (e.g. EPICA Dome C, Vostok or the LR04 benthic stack), edit REFERENCE in app.js.
